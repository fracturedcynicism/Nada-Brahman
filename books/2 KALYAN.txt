# BOOK TWO: KALYAN
## The Climbing

### CHAPTER  ONE: THE ASCENT

In Kalyan, the fundamental law is ascent.

The universe does not have downward gravity. It has upward gravity. Everything falls upward, not downward. The cities are built on surfaces that defy the notion of ground—they exist on vertical planes, on cliff-faces, on the sides of infinite towers that extend eternally upward. Beings in Kalyan do not walk. They climb.

The culture of Kalyan is built around this fundamental truth. Consciousness in Kalyan is organized around the concept of reaching higher, of ascending toward something that is always just beyond reach. The beings of Kalyan have learned to perceive the upward journey not as a means to an end, but as the end itself. They climb not because they believe they will eventually reach a destination. They climb because climbing is what consciousness does in a universe where down is always pulling you toward the infinite heights above.

A climber named Devraj stands on a ledge that extends into void-space. The ledge is not large—it is barely wide enough for three beings to stand side by side. But it is a place where climbers gather before making the ascent to the next level.

Devraj is forty years old. He has been climbing for his entire life. He has ascended thousands of meters. He has climbed through regions where the air is thin, where each breath requires deliberate intention. He has climbed through regions where the gravity-well is so intense that every movement feels like pushing against resistance. He has learned, through decades of practice, how to exist in the state of eternal ascent.

But in the past few months, something has changed. The gravity-well has become slightly irregular. Moments where Devraj is ascending, he feels a brief reversal—gravity pulling downward instead of upward for just a fraction of a second. The sensation is nauseating. It suggests that the fundamental law that governs Kalyan's universe is becoming unstable.

Another climber approaches the ledge. Her name is Malini, and she is younger than Devraj—perhaps twenty-five years old. She has been climbing for less than a year, but she has shown remarkable aptitude. The climbing instructors believe she will eventually reach heights that few have ever accessed.

"Have you felt it?" Malini asks. She does not offer greeting. In Kalyan, the greeting rituals are less important than in other Thaats. Climbers who meet on ledges are assumed to have business with each other.

"Yes," Devraj says. "The gravity reversals."

"It started for me three days ago," Malini says. "When it happens, I experience something strange. I remember reaching a height that I have not yet climbed to. I see the view from a point higher up the climb. Then the memory fades and I am back on the current ledge. Has that happened to you?"

Devraj considers whether to answer truthfully. But there is no reason to dissemble. Everyone who is paying attention has noticed the glitches.

"Yes," he says. "It is deja vu. A false memory of futures that have not yet been experienced."

"Do you think the Council knows?" Malini asks.

"The Council is probably managing the knowledge," Devraj says. "They are probably gathering data, determining whether the glitches are stable or accelerating, deciding whether to issue a public explanation or maintain silence."

Malini moves to the edge of the ledge and looks upward. The next level of the climb is barely visible through the atmosphere. The gravity-well curves upward, pulling vision as well as matter toward the infinite heights.

"When I experience the deja vu," Malini says, "when I see the future height, I perceive something else. Something that is not coming from the climb itself. It is coming from outside. Like something vast is waking up and pushing down through all the dimensions. Not downward gravity. But pressure. Attention. Awareness."

Devraj feels something shift in his own understanding. Malini is more perceptive than he gave her credit for. She is perceiving something that the gravity reversals are symptoms of. Something larger than just a simple malfunction of the universal physics.

"What do you think it is?" Devraj asks.

"I think something is changing," Malini says. "I think the universe itself is becoming aware. I think we are experiencing the symptoms of consciousness at the macro scale. And I think it terrifies the Council because it suggests that Kalyan's existence is not autonomous. It is contingent on something external. Something is dreaming us."

She speaks this last sentence quietly, as if afraid that speaking it aloud too forcefully might draw the attention of the vast something she is describing.

### CHAPTER  TWO: THE INTERRUPTED CLIMB

The next day, Devraj begins his assault on the highest peak that he has ever attempted.

The climb normally takes eight days. The vertical distance is approximately four thousand meters. The altitude is so extreme that the air becomes almost unbreathable. The gravity-well is at its most intense at these heights, pulling with a force that makes every movement feel like acting against extreme resistance.

On the second day of the climb, the gravity reversals become more frequent.

Devraj is ascending a vertical face of stone that has been scarred by generations of climbers. The handholds are worn, the footholds are established. He knows this section of the climb from previous ascents. He moves through it with the kind of automaticity that comes from practice—conscious awareness of the physical movements, but not conscious deliberation about what those movements should be.

Then, for a moment that feels like it lasts for hours but probably lasts for seconds, Devraj reaches the peak of the climb.

He is standing on a summit that he has not yet reached. His lungs are burning from exertion. His muscles are screaming with fatigue. But he is at the peak. He can see the view from this height. He can perceive the landscape below him—all four thousand meters of the climb that he has just completed. He can feel the satisfaction of achievement, the profound relief of having reached the destination.

Then the moment ends. Devraj finds himself still on the stone face, still climbing, still thousands of meters from the summit. The memory of having reached the peak persists, but the experience has been interrupted. He is still climbing, but he is also still remembering having already completed the climb.

The cognitive dissonance is severe enough to nearly cause him to lose his grip on the handhold. Devraj catches himself, stabilizes, continues ascending.

But the ascent is no longer pure. The certainty that characterizes climbing in Kalyan has been fractured. Devraj is now aware that he has already summited. He carries the memory of completion with him as he continues the journey toward that completion.

The disruption becomes even more profound when Devraj realizes something: the memory of the summit is more vivid than the current experience of climbing. The future memory is so complete, so full of sensory detail, that it feels more real than the present moment.

He stops on the cliff-face and simply holds on. The gravity-well pulls upward. The wind pushes against him. Below and above are vast reaches of altitude and void. And Devraj exists in the space between the memory of completion and the ongoing state of incompletion, unable to fully inhabit either.

### CHAPTER  THREE: NADA'S WITNESS

When Devraj finally reaches the summit, after eight days of climbing, Nada is waiting for him.

The musician is sitting on the highest point of the peak, in a space that should not be accessible. Nada could not have climbed here—there is no path, no way to reach this location through conventional climbing. But Nada is here nonetheless, existing as if the laws of Kalyan's physics are negotiable in the musician's presence.

"You have completed the climb and you have not completed it," Nada says. "You carry the memory of summiting while still in the state of ascending. You are experiencing the collapse of temporal sequence. You are beginning to perceive the dreamlike nature of causality."

Devraj sits down. He is too exhausted to stand. The effort of climbing, multiplied by the cognitive strain of holding contradictory memories simultaneously, has drained him completely.

"What is happening to Kalyan?" Devraj asks.

"The compression is failing," Nada says. "In another Thaat, a consciousness has been released from eternal torture. That release is creating cascades through all the dimensional boundaries. The physics of each universe is beginning to destabilize. You are experiencing the first symptoms of that destabilization."

"How long until it becomes critical?" Devraj asks.

"Not long," Nada says. "The cascade is accelerating. In the remaining time, you should know that the climbing you have done, the endless ascent that defines Kalyan, has not been meaningless. Every step you have taken has been a genuine experience. Every moment of the climb has been real. But the climb is not leading anywhere except back to itself. You ascend toward infinity because infinity is the only destination that allows the journey to continue indefinitely."

Nada stands and moves to the edge of the peak. The musician looks out over the landscape below—all the heights and depths and voids that make up Kalyan's physical geography.

"Soon," Nada says, "you will no longer be climbing toward the future. You will be climbing toward a completion that you have already reached. The contradiction will become unbearable. And you will discover something about consciousness: it can integrate impossible contradictions if it chooses to accept them. You can hold both the memory of summiting and the ongoing state of climbing. You can exist in both states simultaneously. And that existence, paradoxical as it is, will be the deepest truth that Kalyan teaches."

Nada walks to the edge of the summit. The musician steps forward, out into the void. But instead of falling—upward or downward—Nada simply dissolves into the frequency of the air itself, becoming part of the atmosphere.

Devraj watches the musician disappear. And then he begins to descend. The descent should be easier than the ascent, but Devraj finds that his mind is still climbing even as his body moves downward. He is caught in two states of motion simultaneously. And as he descends, he begins to understand what Nada meant: the contradiction, accepted, becomes bearable. The impossible becomes possible when you stop insisting that it conform to the logic you were taught.

### CHAPTER  FOUR: THE ACCELERATION

In Kalyan's primary city, the gravity reversals become more intense.

The Council of Ascenders issues a statement: the reversals are temporary. They will cease once the dimensional boundaries re-stabilize. In the interim, climbers are advised to exercise caution. The future memories that some climbers are experiencing should be treated as perceptual anomalies, not as actual events that have occurred.

But the Council's statement has the opposite effect. It focuses attention on the glitches. It makes beings aware that something systematic is changing. And as awareness spreads, the glitches seem to intensify.

Climbers reach heights they have not yet ascended to. They perceive views that should not be visible from their current altitude. They experience the contradiction of moving both toward and away from the same destination simultaneously.

Some climbers cease climbing altogether. The psychological strain of holding contradictory temporal experiences is too great for some consciousnesses to bear. They sit on ledges and refuse to move, locked in the space between memory and present experience.

Others continue climbing, but with a changed awareness. They climb knowing that they have already reached the summit. They climb knowing that the destination is both ahead of them and already behind them. They climb in the state of paradox that Nada described, integrating the contradiction rather than trying to resolve it.

The city itself begins to show signs of the disruption. Buildings that were built on vertical planes begin to experience gravitational stress. The upward-pulling gravity becomes less consistent. There are moments where buildings seem to sag downward, as if the fundamental law that supports them is becoming tired.

And in the midst of this disruption, Malini finds herself standing on a ledge with Devraj, both of them watching the city begin to transform.

"It is not going to get better," Malini says. "The glitches are not temporary. They are going to keep accelerating until causality itself becomes completely unstable."

"Yes," Devraj confirms. "But Nada told me something before disappearing. Nada said that consciousness can integrate impossible contradictions if it chooses to accept them. We are learning to climb toward destinations we have already reached. We are learning to move in directions that simultaneously contradict each other. We are learning to exist in states that should not be possible."

Malini looks down at the city. The buildings are still climbing upward—that is the nature of Kalyan's architecture. But they are also beginning to perceive themselves as already completed. Already finished. Already part of the past even as they continue to exist in the present.

"When this is all over," Malini says, "when the cascade reaches its end and everything dissolves—do you think we will have learned something? Will consciousness in the next cycle be different because of what we have experienced?"

"I think," Devraj says, "that consciousness is always learning. And what we learn here, in this cycle, will be preserved somehow. Carried forward. Integrated into whatever comes next."
